\subsection{Summary of contributions}
We extended a Rybak-style two-legged spinal central pattern generator (CPG)
with four mechanisms absent from the canonical Hodgkin--Huxley lineage
(Rybak 2006 $\to$ Shevtsova 2015 $\to$ Danner 2017 $\to$
Zhang 2022)~\cite{rybak2006,shevtsova2015,danner2017,zhang2022}: (i)
spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) that lets the network tune its own
sensory-to-rhythm-generator synapses rather than have them hand-set; (ii) a
closed loop from muscle-force feedback (Ia) into the reciprocal-inhibition
core; (iii) motoneuron pools driving explicit muscle proxies with force and
length dynamics; and (iv) an externally paced cutaneous drive that emulates
heel-to-toe pressure transfer during stance. Using this model we asked how a
spinal CPG's own synaptic weights should be organised to produce a stable
walking rhythm, and how that self-tuned rhythm degrades as sensory feedback
is progressively withdrawn --- the central manipulation in rodent and human
studies of spinal-cord-injury (SCI) rehabilitation.

Three findings anchor the paper. First, the three plastic synapses --- the
cutaneous CUT$\to$RG-E projection and the homonymous proprioceptive
projections Ia-E$\to$RG-E, Ia-F$\to$RG-F --- converge to the same steady
strength regardless of where they start, of which leg they are in, and of
walking speed (\autoref{sec:results:weights}); the network organises its own
descending gain rather than requiring it to be prescribed by the modeller.
Second, across the full grid of five locomotion modes and five learning
rates the quality of the alternating gait is not maximised by the fastest
learning, but peaks at an intermediate rate and falls off at both extremes
(\autoref{sec:results:comparison}). Third, removing load-dependent sensory
feedback and removing the cutaneous pacing drive have dissociable effects:
a model with only the pacing drive intact keeps stepping right through full
unloading, while a model in which both proprioceptive and cutaneous
feedback are gated by load collapses --- reproducing, in a spiking
closed-loop network, the clinical dissociation between unassisted and
epidurally-stimulated stepping after motor-complete SCI
(\autoref{sec:results:epidural}).

\subsection{Relation to Rybak-lineage spinal CPG models}
Zhang et al.\ (2022)~\cite{zhang2022} name two limitations of their own
four-rhythm-generator model explicitly: it has no biomechanics or sensory
feedback, and it has no motoneurons, treating rhythm-generator output as a
direct stand-in for motor output. Every synaptic weight in their model,
like its Rybak/Shevtsova/Danner predecessors, is hand-tuned. Our model
addresses both limitations for a single hindlimb pair: the Ia force/length
feedback loop supplies the missing sensory closed loop, and explicit
motoneuron pools driving a saturating muscle model supply the missing motor
periphery. On top of this we add a mechanism none of these models have ---
activity-dependent plasticity on the afferent synapses --- so that the
descending and cutaneous gains are discovered by the network rather than
fixed by the experimenter.

The trade-off is architectural detail. Zhang et al.\ resolve genetically
identified commissural and long-propriospinal interneuron classes (V0V,
V0D, V1, V2a, V3, Shox2) across four limbs and reproduce V3-silencing
experiments quantitatively; our model collapses the commissural pathway to
two hand-set inhibitory projections between homologous half-centres and
covers only one limb pair. We also do not reproduce gait-transition
phenomena (walk $\to$ trot $\to$ gallop $\to$ bound): our gait is
externally paced rather than emergent from a single tonic-drive parameter.
The two efforts are complementary rather than competing: theirs
demonstrates architectural realism at the cost of a closed loop; ours
demonstrates a closed, self-organising loop at the cost of architectural
detail. A natural synthesis --- hand-curated interlimb architecture wrapped
in a plastic, closed sensorimotor loop --- is a direct extension of both.

\subsection{Why an intermediate learning rate and an intermediate load are
both optimal}
Two of our findings share the same underlying mechanism, which is worth
making explicit before turning to its clinical reading. Learning only
improves the gait once it has had time to organise the cutaneous weight
appropriately, and only within a specific range of final weight: at the
fastest rate ($\lambda=10^{-2}$) the descending weight overshoots useful
values and the alternation becomes slightly noisier, while at the slower
rates ($\lambda=10^{-5}, 10^{-6}$) the weight never gets there at all, so
the comparison across learning rates (\autoref{sec:results:comparison})
shows a broad optimum around $\lambda=10^{-3}$--$10^{-4}$ rather than a
monotonic benefit of faster learning. The same logic explains the
non-monotonic recovery we observe from toe to air stepping at slow learning
rates: an intermediate, partially-developed weight is poorly enough timed
to \emph{interfere} with the paced core's own alternation, whereas a fully
withdrawn afferent drive (air stepping) simply leaves the paced core to run
on its own, unperturbed floor. In both the learning-rate axis and the
loading axis, then, \emph{some} plastic drive organised correctly is better
than none, but a large, poorly-organised drive can be worse than either
extreme.

\subsection{Clinical implications for spinal cord injury rehabilitation}
Beyond recapitulating locomotor rhythmicity, the model offers a mechanistic
account of how sensorimotor interaction and activity-dependent plasticity
could support functional recovery after SCI, and several of its
quantitative findings map directly onto clinical observations.

\paragraph{Sensory input as a catalyst for plasticity, not a replacement for
descending drive.} In our model the brainstem input is held fixed and weak
throughout; every gain in gait quality comes from tuning the
\emph{afferent} synapses. Weight-bearing locomotion drives strong,
consistent potentiation of the cutaneous projection across the loaded
modes and toe stepping ($\mathrm{CUT}\to\mathrm{RG\text{-}E}\approx$
55--65\,pA at the fast and intermediate learning rates), whereas full
unloading suppresses that potentiation almost entirely ($\approx4$\,pA at
the slowest rates) and yields a markedly less organised
gait (\autoref{sec:results:weights}, \autoref{sec:results:comparison}).
Load- and activity-appropriate sensory input is therefore not a substitute
for the lost supraspinal drive in this model; it is what the spinal network
uses to reorganise itself. This parallels the clinical observation that
task-specific gait training, robot-assisted locomotion, and body-weight-supported
treadmill training improve walking performance by engaging residual
plasticity in spinal circuitry rather than by regenerating damaged
descending pathways\todo{cite task-specific / robot-assisted / BWSTT gait
training literature}.

\paragraph{A mechanistic hypothesis for epidural stimulation.} The paired
loading contrast (\autoref{sec:results:epidural}) gives this a direct
reading in terms of assisted stepping. When cutaneous input is gated by
loading along with everything else (the unassisted case), alternation
collapses under full unloading (corr$(F_E,F_F)=-0.41$, with the cycle
period becoming irregular); when the same rhythmic cutaneous drive is held
on regardless of load --- the model's analogue of epidural spinal cord
stimulation (ESCS), which delivers a rhythmic afferent volley independent
of limb contact --- alternation is preserved across the entire loading
range ($-0.94$ at full weight-bearing to $-0.97$ at full unloading). This
double dissociation offers a candidate mechanism for the clinical benefit
of ESCS in motor-complete SCI~\cite{harkema2011,courtine2009,edgerton2008,
lavrov2008}: rather than (or in addition to) simply raising spinal
excitability, rhythmic epidural stimulation may act by restoring the
timed sensory volley that the spinal CPG needs to organise alternating
output, in effect substituting for the loading-dependent afferent signal
that unassisted stepping has lost. If this mechanism holds, neuromodulation
protocols that pair electrical stimulation with functionally patterned
sensory input --- rather than tonic or unpatterned stimulation alone ---
would be expected to engage the spinal locomotor circuitry more
effectively.

\paragraph{An intermediate degree of plasticity, not the maximum, supports
the best gait.} The learning-rate sweep (\autoref{sec:results:comparison})
shows that the most robust, cleanly-alternating gait is produced by an
intermediate learning rate ($\lambda\approx10^{-3}$--$10^{-4}$); both
slower and --- more surprisingly --- the very fastest learning rate we
tested give a noisier or less-converged result. If a similar
weakest-at-the-extremes relationship holds between plasticity and recovery
in vivo, it offers a heuristic for titrating rehabilitation intensity or
the dose of pharmacological and electrical neuromodulation therapies aimed
at spinal adaptation: the goal would be to find the degree of induced
plasticity that lets spinal circuits reorganise without destabilising them
through excessive rewiring, and that optimum would plausibly need to be
set individually, given how much a single parameter's value shifted the
outcome in our sweep.

\paragraph{An in-silico platform for designing interventions.} Because
every manipulation in this model --- loading level, learning rate,
stimulation pattern, which synapses are plastic --- is an explicit,
inspectable parameter, the same framework could be used prospectively:
running simulated variants of robot-assisted training at different
assistance levels, ESCS at different sites, frequencies, or timings
relative to the gait cycle, and combinations of the two, to narrow the
candidate space before a clinical trial or a specific patient protocol is
committed to. Coupled with patient-specific data this points toward a
``virtual twin'' use case --- simulating, and eventually helping to select
among, candidate rehabilitation strategies for an individual before they
are applied.

\subsection{Limitations}
The model trades biological detail for a tractable, fully closed
sensorimotor loop, and several simplifications bound how far its
quantitative predictions should be read.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Abstracted sensorimotor pathways.} Interneuron populations,
        proprioceptive transduction, and motor output are represented at a
        level of abstraction chosen for tractability, not anatomical
        completeness: we resolve two hand-set commissural projections
        rather than the genetically identified V0/V1/V2a/V3 interneuron
        classes, and we model one hindlimb pair rather than
        fore--hindlimb coordination~\cite{zhang2022}.
  \item \textbf{No supraspinal control, fatigue, or longer-term structural
        plasticity.} The brainstem drive is a fixed tonic input, not an
        adaptive descending controller; muscle fatigue and slower forms of
        structural plasticity beyond STDP are not modelled.
  \item \textbf{No SCI pathology.} The model represents reduced
        \emph{sensory drive}, not the injury itself: chronic dorsal- or
        ventral-root damage, glial scarring, and the substantial
        heterogeneity of human SCI presentations are not represented.
  \item \textbf{Not calibrated to patient or animal data.} No experimental
        recordings from an SCI preparation were used to fit any parameter,
        including the STDP rate and weight ceilings. The model should
        therefore be read as hypothesis-generating --- a candidate
        mechanism and a set of qualitative and quantitative predictions to
        be tested --- rather than as a validated predictive tool.
        Establishing greater biological realism will require incorporating
        more detailed muscle dynamics, more realistic sensory transduction,
        an explicit supraspinal controller, and eventually patient-specific
        data.
  \item \textbf{Izhikevich, not Hodgkin--Huxley.} We trade biophysical
        detail for simulation efficiency; persistent-sodium
        (\textit{I}$_\text{NaP}$)-like bursting is approximated by the
        intrinsic-bursting Izhikevich parameter set rather than derived
        from channel kinetics, so the model is not appropriate for
        questions specific to \textit{I}$_\text{NaP}$ pharmacology.
  \item \textbf{No stochastic replication.} Results are deterministic,
        fixed-seed point estimates; the ten-point initial-weight sweep
        (\autoref{sec:results:weights}) substitutes for repeated stochastic
        trials in establishing that a result is not an artefact of a
        particular initialisation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Concluding remark}
A Rybak-style spinal half-centre oscillator, closed in the loop with
motoneurons, muscle proxies, and load-dependent sensory feedback, tunes its
own descending gain through spike-timing-dependent plasticity to a stable
locomotor attractor --- one that is largely independent of how the network
starts, robust across the rat locomotor speed range, and best expressed at
an intermediate degree of plasticity rather than the maximum
achievable. Withdrawing load-dependent sensory feedback degrades that gait,
and restoring a rhythmic, load-independent afferent volley --- the model's
analogue of epidural stimulation --- rescues it, reproducing a central
dissociation in the SCI rehabilitation literature within a single
mechanistic, closed-loop model. Beyond the specific findings, the model
demonstrates that a spinal CPG's descending and sensory gains need not be
prescribed by the modeller: given a plastic synapse, appropriately timed
sensory drive, and enough time, the network finds them on its own. That
property, together with the model's explicit and inspectable parameters,
is what makes it a plausible substrate for exploring --- and eventually,
with patient-specific calibration, for helping to design --- sensorimotor
rehabilitation strategies after spinal cord injury.
