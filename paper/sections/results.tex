\subsection{Self-organisation of the three plastic projections}\label{sec:results:weights}

\paragraph{The canonical model and its rehabilitation modes.}
All results use the canonical sensory-sited model: the brainstem drive
BS$\to$RG is frozen and the plasticity lives in three projections --- the
cutaneous CUT$\to$RG-E and the two homonymous proprioceptive projections
Ia-E$\to$RG-E and Ia-F$\to$RG-F. Every result is organised on the same
$5\times3$ grid of \emph{rehabilitation modes}: five locomotion modes --- slow
walk (6\,cm/s), medium walk $=$ plantar stepping $=$ baseline loading
(13.5\,cm/s), fast walk (21\,cm/s), toe stepping (partial unloading) and air
stepping (full unloading) --- crossed with three STDP learning rates
$\lambda\in\{10^{-3},10^{-4},10^{-5}\}$.

\paragraph{Weights converge symmetrically to a mode-independent set-point.}
Figure~\ref{fig:weight_matrix} shows the weight trajectory of each plastic
projection, for both legs, across the five locomotion modes (the three learning
rates overlaid in each panel). The cutaneous CUT$\to$RG-E projection converges
to $\approx63$\,pA in every loaded mode, while the two proprioceptive
projections self-stabilise at a low $\approx4$--$5$\,pA set-point, well below
their $10$\,pA cap --- the light, phased Ia feedback that reinforces each burst
without filling the inter-burst trough. The left and right legs are
indistinguishable.

  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{fig_stdp_weight_matrix.png}
  \caption{\textbf{STDP weight trajectories of the three plastic projections

\paragraph{Learning rate sets the time-to-plateau; unloading slows it.}
Broken out per learning rate (Figure~\ref{fig:weights_grid}), CUT$\to$RG-E
reaches its plateau within $\approx7$\,s at $\lambda=10^{-3}$, $\approx75$\,s at
$10^{-4}$, and does not converge within the $120$\,s run at $10^{-5}$. Under
progressive unloading the potentiation slows: at air stepping even the fast
rate only climbs gradually (reaching $\approx50$\,pA at $t=120$\,s), because the
loading-gated afferents that drive the learning are attenuated. The two
proprioceptive projections stay at their low set-point throughout.

\subsection{Rehabilitation progress: force develops as the weight converges}\label{sec:results:force}

Figures~\ref{fig:force_l3}--\ref{fig:force_l5} track the extensor/flexor muscle
force in three windows of the run --- beginning ($t\approx4$--$9$\,s), middle
($40$--$45$\,s) and end ($115$--$120$\,s) --- for each locomotion mode, one
figure per learning rate. As the CUT$\to$RG-E weight grows, the counter-phase
force pattern sharpens from ragged early bursts into a clean antiphase. The
progression completes in the loaded modes (slow, medium and fast walk reach
corr$(F_E,F_F)\le-0.96$), degrades gracefully under toe stepping ($-0.82$), and
fails under air stepping ($-0.51$): there the gated afferents keep CUT$\to$RG-E
weak ($\approx9$\,pA even at $t=120$\,s) and the extensor force never fully
develops. The learning rate sets how much of the run is spent in the
under-developed regime --- at $\lambda=10^{-3}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:force_l3}) the
rhythm is essentially converged by the first window, whereas at $10^{-5}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:force_l5}) it remains under-developed throughout.

\subsection{Network-level activity across the locomotion modes}\label{sec:results:network}

Figures~\ref{fig:net_l3}--\ref{fig:net_l5} show the population firing rates of
all sixteen recorded groups --- both legs' RG-F/RG-E rhythm generators, InF/InE
reciprocal-inhibition interneurons, IaInt-F/IaInt-E proprioceptive interneurons
and M-F/M-E motor pools --- across the five locomotion modes, one figure per
learning rate. Through slow, medium and fast walk and under toe stepping, every
flexor-side population fires in strict antiphase to its extensor-side
counterpart, the two legs hold a $180^{\circ}$ offset (right-leg rows tinted),
and the reciprocal In and IaInt interneurons carry the same clean alternation as
the rhythm generators --- so the inhibitory core, not merely the motor read-out,
is phase-locked. Under air stepping (rightmost column) the loading-gated
afferent drive is withdrawn and the extensor-side populations (RG-E, InE,
IaInt-E, M-E) collapse while the intrinsically-bursting flexor side persists ---
the population-level signature of the unloading failure.

\subsection{Epidural stimulation rescues stepping under unloading}\label{sec:results:epidural}

Figure~\ref{fig:epidural} contrasts the two loading paradigms. In the natural
(unassisted) case the cutaneous and proprioceptive drives are gated together by
loading, and the rhythm collapses as the limb is unloaded (corr $-0.52$ at air
stepping). When the paced cutaneous drive is instead held intact regardless of
loading --- the computational analogue of epidural lumbar stimulation, which
supplies a rhythmic afferent volley independent of limb
contact~\cite{courtine2009,harkema2011} --- the counter-phase is preserved
across the entire loading range (corr $\approx-0.98$). This double dissociation
reproduces the mechanism underlying the clinical observation that motor-complete
subjects cannot step unassisted but recover treadmill stepping under epidural
stimulation~\cite{courtine2009,harkema2011}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{fig9_epidural_contrast.png}
  \caption{\textbf{Epidural-stimulation rescue --- paced cutaneous drive
    preserves stepping under unloading.} Loading swept from baseline to air
    stepping. \textbf{(a)} counter-phase corr$(F_E,F_F)$ versus loading:
    epidural-stim (CUT held intact) stays strong, the natural (CUT gated) arm
    collapses. \textbf{(b)} cycle-period jitter versus loading.
    \textbf{(c, d)} representative air-stepping force traces for the stim and
    natural arms.}
  \label{fig:epidural}
\end{figure}
