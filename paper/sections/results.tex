\subsection{Self-organisation of locomotor pattern via STDP}\label{sec:results:stdp}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
Across all ten initial-weight points
$(\mu,\mathrm{CV}) \in \{(0,0),\,(0.5,0.8),\,(1.0,0.6),\,(2.0,0.45),\,
(3.5,0.30),\,(5.0,0.20),\,(7.0,0.15),\,(9.0,0.10),\,(12.0,0.08),\,
(16.0,0.05)\}$, all three plastic projections (CUT$\to$RG-E, BS$\to$RG-E,
BS$\to$RG-F) converge to the same steady-state weight distribution by
$t \approx 5{-}10\,\mathrm{s}$ and remain stable for the full
\SI{120}{\second} of simulation.

\subsection{Long-term stability of counter-phase locomotion}\label{sec:results:stability}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
Over \SI{120}{\second} (120 gait cycles) at the production
configuration ($\mu=3.5$, $\mathrm{CV}=0.30$, BS\,$=60\,$Hz, N$=$200 per
half-centre), Force-E peaks at \SI{17.4}{\au}, Force-F at
\SI{17.4}{\au}, with a Force-E$\leftrightarrow$Force-F Pearson
correlation of $-0.91$ on the last 20\,s window. Cycle period jitter
is $<\SI{1}{\milli\second}$.

\subsection{Speed by STDP matrix: gait tracks commanded step period across the rat locomotor range}\label{sec:results:speed}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
We tested all nine combinations of three walking speeds
(Courtine/Lavrov rat treadmill
range~\cite{lavrov2008,courtine2009}: $\approx$\,6, 13.5, 21\,cm/s,
corresponding to step periods of 1200, 520, 350\,ms with $\approx$\,7\,cm
step length) crossed with three STDP learning rates spanning a decade
around the bio-plausible cortical
value~\cite{biPoo1998,morrison2007}
($\lambda \in \{1{\times}10^{-4},\,1{\times}10^{-3},\,1{\times}10^{-2}\}$).
The measured cycle period tracks the commanded period within $\le 1$\,ms
in 7 of 9 cells; E$\leftrightarrow$F counter-phase strength stays in
$[-0.90, -0.74]$ across the full $3 \times 3$ matrix
(Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_stdp}, Table~\ref{tab:speed_stdp_metrics}).

\paragraph{Interpretation: gait tracking is asymptotically
rate-invariant.}
The measured cycle period equals the commanded step period to within
the sampling resolution (sub-millisecond) across all nine cells, with
the only exception being the fastest gait (350\,ms commanded period)
where cycle jitter rises to $\approx 11$\,ms\,s.d.\ at the slower
learning rates. This is consistent with the rat fast-twitch fibre
rise-time limit ($\tau_F=80$\,ms) becoming a non-negligible fraction of
the 175\,ms stance window: Hill-style saturation~\cite{zajac1989}
prevents force from fully developing every stride. Peak Force-E
correspondingly drops from $17.46$ to $15.47$\,a.u.\ as the step period
shortens from 1200 to 350\,ms --- a $\approx 11\%$ compression.

\paragraph{Interpretation: $\lambda$ acts on the transient, not the
asymptote.}
The time-domain summary heat-tiles (Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_stdp}, Row 4)
are nearly flat across $\lambda$: by $t=120$\,s, all three learning
rates have reached the same converged weight distribution, and the
externally clocked paced gait masks any residual variability. The
$\lambda$-effect is instead visible in the convergence transient
(Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_stdp}, Row 5, panels d and e), for which we
deliberately widened $\lambda$ to span a full decade
($1{\times}10^{-4}$ to $1{\times}10^{-2}$): at the fast rate
($\lambda=1{\times}10^{-2}$) the CUT$\to$RG-E weight reaches its plateau
within $\sim$1\,s, at the baseline rate ($\lambda=1{\times}10^{-3}$)
within $\sim$7\,s, and at the slow rate ($\lambda=1{\times}10^{-4}$)
only after $\sim$60--75\,s. The BS$\to$RG-E weight reaches its
$w_{\max}=30$\,pA ceiling in roughly the same ratio. We deliberately
ran 120\,s simulations so that even the slow rate converges within the
run, demonstrating the post-convergence stability claim of
\autoref{sec:results:stability}; the functional consequence of the
slower transient is exposed in Phase~B
(\autoref{sec:results:ablation}), where attenuating the closed-loop Ia
makes the rhythm depend on how quickly the descending weights can
adapt.

\paragraph{Interpretation: spectral concentration as a sensitive
irregularity metric.}
Cycle-period s.d. is essentially zero for the externally paced rhythm
($\ll$\,1\,ms in 7 of 9 cells), and is therefore an insensitive index
of irregularity. The spectral concentration (Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_stdp},
panel f) measures the fraction of total PSD power within $\pm$10\% of
the fundamental cycle frequency $f_0=1/P$. It rises monotonically from
$\approx 0.86$ at slow walk to $\approx 0.95$ at fast walk, reflecting
the increasing concentration of power at the fundamental as the rhythm
locks more tightly to the external clock at higher cadence; within each
speed it also edges up slightly with $\lambda$ (e.g.\ $0.92 \to 0.95$
at fast walk from slow to fast STDP). Importantly, this metric does not
saturate at the converged plateau and so provides a finer-grained
diagnostic than period s.d.\ for future studies that probe
sub-millisecond timing.

\paragraph{Interpretation: bio-plausible mapping.}
Our three speed conditions cover the canonical rat treadmill locomotor
range used in the spinal cord injury literature: 6\,cm/s (slow
exploration; Lavrov 2008~\cite{lavrov2008}), 13.5\,cm/s (medium walk;
Courtine 2009~\cite{courtine2009}), and 21\,cm/s (fast walk / slow trot
transition). Counter-phase correlation remains strong ($\le -0.74$)
across the full range, indicating that paced sensory drive supports the
rat locomotor speed range without retuning of the central architecture.

\subsection{Graded sensory ablation by STDP matrix:\\ STDP rescues air stepping}\label{sec:results:ablation}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
To emulate the Courtine/Lavrov spinal cord injury (SCI) rehabilitation
paradigm~\cite{lavrov2008,courtine2009,edgerton2008}, we tested three
Ia feedback gains ($G_{\text{Ia}} \in \{1.0,\,0.5,\,0.1\}$,
corresponding to \emph{baseline}, \emph{toe stepping}, and \emph{air
stepping}) crossed with the same decade-spanning STDP rates as Phase A
($\lambda \in \{1{\times}10^{-4},\,1{\times}10^{-3},\,1{\times}10^{-2}\}$),
at the medium-walk anchor (520\,ms cycle period, 120\,s simulations so
that even the slow rate converges). Three findings emerge
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ablgrad}, Table~\ref{tab:ablgrad_metrics}):
(i) the baseline gait is robust to $\lambda$ at $G_{\text{Ia}}=1.0$
(519.6\,ms, corr $\approx -0.8$ throughout);
(ii) graded Ia loss introduces cycle-period jitter, most severely at the
slow learning rate (air stepping, $\lambda=10^{-4}$: $480.6 \pm 98.6$\,ms,
corr $-0.73$);
(iii) raising $\lambda$ rescues the degraded conditions --- at air
stepping, the counter-phase correlation strengthens from $-0.73$
($\lambda=10^{-4}$) through $-0.92$ ($\lambda=10^{-3}$) to $-0.94$
($\lambda=10^{-2}$).

\paragraph{Interpretation: graded Ia loss progressively destabilises
the rhythm.}
Reducing Ia gain introduces cycle-period jitter that is most severe at
the slow learning rate. At the slow rate ($\lambda=10^{-4}$), the cycle
s.d.\ grows from $0$ (baseline) to $61.3$\,ms (toe stepping) to
$98.6$\,ms (air stepping), while the counter-phase correlation weakens
from $-0.78$ to $-0.72$ to $-0.73$. The peak force is largely
preserved ($\approx 16.7$--$17.0$\,a.u.) throughout, so the degradation
is one of \emph{timing} rather than amplitude. This gradation mirrors
experimental findings in SCI-rehabilitation studies where
progressively unloading the hindlimbs degrades step rhythm
quality~\cite{lavrov2008,edgerton2008,hagglund2013}.

\paragraph{What our ``ablation'' actually maps to: epidural-stimulation
paradigm.}
A crucial framing point: the \verb|--ia-feedback-gain| knob attenuates
only the \emph{closed-loop, force-driven} Ia rate; the externally paced
cutaneous CUT pattern and the sequential Ia-E heel$\to$mid$\to$toe
ramp remain at full amplitude in all three ablation conditions. The
paced external drive in our model is therefore the computational
analogue of \emph{epidural electrical
stimulation}~\cite{lavrov2008,courtine2009,harkema2011}, which
supplies a rhythmic afferent volley to the spinal cord independently
of intact sensory feedback. Phase B is thus not a model of unassisted
deafferentation but of \emph{SCI plus epidural stimulation under
progressively unloaded weight-bearing}. This is precisely the
experimental paradigm pioneered by Lavrov, Courtine, and Edgerton: rats
with complete spinal transection, lying on a treadmill with epidural
stimulation, can step stably from full weight-bearing through partial
weight-bearing to limb-suspended air stepping when stimulation is
intact.

\paragraph{STDP rescues air stepping under epidural-style drive.}
The strongest result of Phase B is along the bottom row of
Fig.~\ref{fig:ablgrad}: at the maximally degraded condition (air
stepping, $G_{\text{Ia}}=0.1$), increasing the STDP rate from
$\lambda=1{\times}10^{-4}$ (slow) through $\lambda=1{\times}10^{-3}$
(baseline) to $\lambda=1{\times}10^{-2}$ (fast) restores the rhythm:
cycle-period s.d.\ falls from $98.6$\,ms to $0$\,ms (baseline rate) and
the E$\leftrightarrow$F counter-phase correlation strengthens from
$-0.73$ to $-0.92$ and then $-0.94$ --- the latter exceeding the
intact, weight-bearing baseline ($-0.78$ to $-0.81$). Reframed in the
epidural-stim paradigm of the previous paragraph, this finding states:
when descending rhythmic drive
(epidural stimulation, in vivo; paced CUT $+$ external Ia-E, in our
model) is intact and synaptic plasticity is sufficiently fast, the
spinal CPG re-weights its descending and cutaneous afferent inputs to
ignore the noisy attenuated closed-loop Ia and lock onto the cleaner
external clock --- producing a rhythm that is in fact \emph{more}
regular than the baseline weight-bearing condition. This matches the
clinical observations of Courtine 2009 and Harkema
2011~\cite{courtine2009,harkema2011}: in chronic SCI rats and humans
with motor-complete paraplegia, epidural lumbar stimulation enables
treadmill stepping that improves over training sessions, suggesting
Hebbian-type plasticity at the descending--spinal interface. We
explicitly do \emph{not} claim that unassisted air stepping (without
any external drive) would yield the same result; testing that would
require switching off the paced cutaneous and external Ia ramps as
well, which we leave for future work.

\subsection{Summary of results}\label{sec:results:summary}

\todo{Brief 1-paragraph synthesis summarising the four main findings:
