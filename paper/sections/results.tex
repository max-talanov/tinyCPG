\subsection{Self-organisation of locomotor pattern via STDP}\label{sec:results:stdp}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
Across ten initialisations of the plastic weights --- a lognormal prior
with $(\mu,\mathrm{CV})$ ranging from $(0,0)$ to $(16,0.05)$, i.e.\ four
orders of magnitude in the initial mean --- STDP drives the cutaneous
CUT$\to$RG-E projection to the \emph{same} converged strength,
$63.6\pm0.3\,$pA (across-initialisation CV $0.4\%$), within
$t\approx7$--$10\,$s, and holds it stable for the full \SI{120}{\second}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:init_convergence}). The brainstem projections likewise
reach a common value independent of the starting weights (BS$\to$RG-E
$15.5\,$pA, BS$\to$RG-F $15.9\,$pA). The converged weights are therefore
set by the closed-loop network dynamics, not by the initialisation: even
starting every synapse at zero ($\mu=0$) the network self-organises to
the identical attractor.

  \caption{\textbf{Initialisation-robustness of STDP self-organisation.}
    \emph{Main idea: the learned synaptic weight is a property of the
    network dynamics, not a value we tune --- whatever the weights start

    bio-plausible defaults. \textbf{(a)} Mean CUT$\to$RG-E weight trajectory for each

  \label{fig:init_convergence}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Quantitative summary.}
The canonical model sites its plasticity in the sensory loop (frozen
BS$\to$RG; plastic CUT$\to$RG-E, Ia-E$\to$RG-E, Ia-F$\to$RG-F). At the
baseline learning rate ($\lambda=10^{-3}$; last-20\,s window,
Fig.~\ref{fig:stdp_matrix}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item CUT$\to$RG-E converges to $\approx63\,$pA --- the single large
        plastic input --- with a fast plateau ($\approx52\,$pA at $t=5\,$s,
        flat by $\approx7\,$s).
  \item The two proprioceptive projections self-stabilise at a low
        set-point: Ia-E$\to$RG-E and Ia-F$\to$RG-F at $\approx4$--$5\,$pA,
        well below their $10\,$pA cap. This low Ia set-point is exactly the
        regime that reinforces each burst without filling the inter-burst
        trough (\autoref{sec:results:sensory}).
  \item Time-to-plateau scales with the learning rate ($\approx7\,$s /
        $\approx75\,$s / not-within-\SI{120}{\second} at
        $\lambda=10^{-3}$/$10^{-4}$/$10^{-5}$), yet the asymptote is common.
\end{itemize}
All three projections behave identically in the left and right legs and
across the five locomotion modes (Fig.~\ref{fig:stdp_matrix}); only under
air stepping does the loading-gated afferent drive visibly slow
CUT$\to$RG-E learning.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
That the converged weights are invariant to \emph{both} the
initialisation (Fig.~\ref{fig:init_convergence}) and the learning rate
--- every starting weight and every $\lambda$ that plateaus reaches the
same CUT$\to$RG-E $\approx63\,$pA and the same $\approx4$--$5\,$pA Ia
set-point --- shows that the cutaneous and proprioceptive synaptic
strengths are fixed points of the closed-loop dynamics rather than free
parameters set by the experimenter. The learning rate governs only the
\emph{time} to reach the attractor, not its location. This is the
self-organisation claim of the paper: a spinal locomotor pattern and the
synaptic weights that support it emerge from STDP acting on the
sensory-entrained rhythm, with no hand-tuning, identically in both legs
and across all five locomotion modes (Fig.~\ref{fig:stdp_matrix}).

\paragraph{Force develops as the weights converge.}
The consequence for motor output is direct
(Fig.~\ref{fig:force_stages}). Sampling the extensor/flexor force in three
windows of the run --- early ($t\approx4$--9\,s), mid ($40$--45\,s) and
late ($115$--120\,s) --- shows the counter-phase waveform sharpen as the
plastic weight grows: early bursts are ragged with phase slips and
amplitude jitter (CUT$\to$RG-E $\approx7$\,pA), whereas the late window is
a clean, regular antiphase (CUT$\to$RG-E $\approx64$\,pA). The same
progression holds across all five learning modes, including the two in
which the plasticity is sited in the proprioceptive loop (the Ia$\to$RG
weight self-stabilises at its low $\approx4$--5\,pA set-point while the
cutaneous drive develops). For this figure the learning rate is
$\lambda=10^{-4}$, chosen so that convergence spans the full run and the
three stages are visibly distinct (at $\lambda=10^{-3}$ convergence is
complete within $\approx8$\,s).

\subsection{Long-term stability of counter-phase locomotion}\label{sec:results:stability}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
Over \SI{120}{\second} at the production configuration ($\mu=3.5$,
$\mathrm{CV}=0.30$, BS\,$=60\,$Hz, N$=$200 per half-centre; medium walk,
520\,ms), Force-E peaks at 16.7\,a.u., Force-F at 16.9\,a.u.,
with a Force-E$\leftrightarrow$Force-F Pearson correlation of $-0.88$ on
the last 20\,s window. Cycle period jitter is $<\SI{1}{\milli\second}$.

\paragraph{Quantitative summary.}
Over the last 20\,s (38 cycles at the 520\,ms medium walk, leg L):
Force-E peaks at 16.72\,a.u. and Force-F at 16.94\,a.u. --- a
balanced half-centre output --- with an E$\leftrightarrow$F Pearson
correlation of $-0.88$ (RG-E$\leftrightarrow$RG-F rate correlation
$-0.96$). The rhythm shows \emph{no drift}: the 95th-percentile Force-E
peak is identical at 20--40\,s and 100--120\,s (16.72\,a.u. in both
windows), and the left and right legs hold a $180^{\circ}$ antiphase
throughout (measured L$\to$R lag $260\,\mathrm{ms}=180^{\circ}$ of the
520\,ms cycle). Cycle-period jitter is below the sampling resolution.

\paragraph{Comparison with Zhang 2022.}
Rybak-lineage half-centre models, including Zhang
2022~\cite{zhang2022}, are typically reported as ${\sim}10$-cycle
averages without a long-term stability quantification. Our
\SI{120}{\second} run --- some $230$ cycles, an order of magnitude
longer --- exhibits no amplitude or period drift, supporting the claim
that the STDP-self-organised weights sit at a genuinely stable attractor
rather than a slowly-drifting transient.

\subsection{Speed by STDP matrix: gait tracks commanded step period across the rat locomotor range}\label{sec:results:speed}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
We tested all nine combinations of three walking speeds
(Courtine/Lavrov rat treadmill
range~\cite{lavrov2008,courtine2009}: $\approx$\,6, 13.5, 21\,cm/s,
corresponding to step periods of 1200, 520, 350\,ms with $\approx$\,7\,cm
step length) crossed with three STDP learning rates spanning two
decades below the bio-plausible cortical
value~\cite{biPoo1998,morrison2007}
($\lambda \in \{1{\times}10^{-5},\,1{\times}10^{-4},\,1{\times}10^{-3}\}$).
The measured cycle period tracks the commanded period within $\le 1$\,ms
in 7 of 9 cells (the two exceptions are both the slowest learning rate,
$\lambda=10^{-5}$, which has not converged within 120\,s), and the
E$\leftrightarrow$F counter-phase is strong throughout
($|\mathrm{corr}| \ge 0.81$). The counter-phase strength depends
\emph{non-monotonically} on $\lambda$: at slow and medium walk it is
strongest at the slowest rate ($\lambda=10^{-5}$, corr down to $-0.99$)
and dips at $\lambda=10^{-4}$, whereas at the fast gait the baseline
rate ($\lambda=10^{-3}$) is strongest ($-0.95$)
(Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_stdp}, Table~\ref{tab:speed_stdp_metrics}).

\paragraph{Interpretation: period tracking and the fast-gait force
compression.}
The measured cycle period equals the commanded step period to within
the sampling resolution (sub-millisecond) across all nine cells, with
the only exception being the fastest gait (350\,ms commanded period)
where cycle jitter rises to $\approx 11$\,ms\,s.d.\ at the converged
learning rates. This is consistent with the rat fast-twitch fibre
rise-time limit ($\tau_F=80$\,ms) becoming a non-negligible fraction of
the 175\,ms stance window: Hill-style saturation~\cite{zajac1989}
prevents force from fully developing every stride. Peak Force-E
correspondingly drops from $17.46$ to $15.5$\,a.u.\ as the step period
shortens from 1200 to 350\,ms --- a $\approx 11\%$ compression.

\paragraph{Interpretation: a non-monotonic, two-regime dependence on
$\lambda$.}
The counter-phase correlation (Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_stdp}, tile c)
depends non-monotonically on the learning rate: at slow and medium walk
it is strongest at the slowest rate ($\lambda=10^{-5}$: $-0.99$ at slow
walk, $-0.93$ at medium walk), weakest at the intermediate rate
($\lambda=10^{-4}$), and recovers at the baseline rate
($\lambda=10^{-3}$); at the fast gait this inverts and the baseline
rate is strongest ($-0.95$). The weight trajectories
(Row 5, panel d; Fig.~\ref{fig:stdp_matrix}) explain why. At
$\lambda=10^{-5}$ the plastic CUT$\to$RG-E weight is still climbing at the
end of the run (medium-walk CUT$\to$RG-E reaches only $\sim 11$\,pA, versus
$63$\,pA when fully converged): the plastic cutaneous pathway is \emph{weak},
so the rhythm is dominated by the externally paced drive and the
reciprocal-inhibition core, which together impose a near-perfect
anti-phase. As $\lambda$ increases and the descending weights reach
full strength, they begin to interact with that core rhythm; at the
intermediate rate the fully-developed but tightly-distributed weights
track the gait less cleanly, producing the counter-phase dip. The
baseline rate recovers a broader, more heterogeneous weight
distribution (quantified in Phase~B,
\autoref{sec:results:ablation}) and with it a clean rhythm. In all
nine cells the counter-phase remains strong ($|\mathrm{corr}|\ge0.81$),
so the gait is robust across the learning-rate range; the
non-monotonicity concerns the \emph{degree} of counter-phase, not its
presence.

\paragraph{Interpretation: the paced core carries gait quality;
plasticity is extensor-biased.}
That the counter-phase is \emph{cleanest at the slowest learning rate}
--- when the descending weights are least developed --- is at first
counterintuitive, but follows from the model's division of labour. The
near-perfect anti-phase is a property of the paced heel$\to$toe drive
and the reciprocal-inhibition core, which are present from $t=0$
independent of learning (the gait alternates cleanly within the first
seconds even at $\lambda=10^{-5}$, before any weight has converged).
The STDP-learned descending drive is an \emph{additional} input layered
on this core, and crucially it is extensor-biased: the only large
plastic projection, CUT$\to$RG-E, targets the extensor and has no
flexor counterpart (\autoref{sec:methods:stdp}). At the medium walk the
converged extensor drive (CUT$+$BS$\to$RG-E $\approx 78$\,pA) dwarfs the
flexor drive (BS$\to$RG-F $\approx 16$\,pA). Strong learning therefore
adds a large, extensor-weighted descending current that perturbs the
balanced paced rhythm, slightly reducing counter-phase (medium walk:
from $-0.93$ at the slowest rate to $-0.88$ at full drive). The same asymmetry leaves the
flexor half-centre, which is intrinsically generated and never receives
a comparable learned drive, visibly more variable than the extensor
across all conditions. We interpret STDP here as self-organising the
\emph{descending drive} --- the quantity that matters for operation
without external pacing and for the epidural-stimulation contrast of
Phase~B --- rather than as the source of counter-phase precision, which
the paced spinal core already provides.

\paragraph{Interpretation: spectral concentration as a sensitive
irregularity metric.}
Cycle-period s.d. is essentially zero for the externally paced rhythm
($\ll$\,1\,ms in 7 of 9 cells), and is therefore an insensitive index
of irregularity. The spectral concentration (Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_stdp},
panel f) measures the fraction of total PSD power within $\pm$10\% of
the fundamental cycle frequency $f_0=1/P$. It rises monotonically from
$\approx 0.86$ at slow walk to $\approx 0.95$ at fast walk, reflecting
the increasing concentration of power at the fundamental as the rhythm
locks more tightly to the external clock at higher cadence; the
dependence on $\lambda$ within a given speed is weak and, like the
counter-phase, non-monotonic. This metric does not saturate at the
converged plateau and so provides a finer-grained diagnostic than
period s.d.\ for future studies that probe sub-millisecond timing.

\paragraph{Interpretation: bio-plausible mapping.}
Our three speed conditions cover the canonical rat treadmill locomotor
range used in the spinal cord injury literature: 6\,cm/s (slow
exploration; Lavrov 2008~\cite{lavrov2008}), 13.5\,cm/s (medium walk;
Courtine 2009~\cite{courtine2009}), and 21\,cm/s (fast walk / slow trot
transition). Counter-phase correlation remains strong ($\le -0.81$)
across the full range, indicating that paced sensory drive supports the
rat locomotor speed range without retuning of the central architecture.

\subsection{Network-level activity: every population alternates in counter-phase across all modes}\label{sec:results:network}

\paragraph{Full-circuit population rates.}
Behind the muscle-force output, the counter-phase organisation is
expressed at every level of the circuit. Figs.~\ref{fig:net_speed}--%
\ref{fig:net_ablsens} show the population firing rates of all sixteen
recorded groups --- both legs' RG-F/RG-E rhythm generators, InF/InE
reciprocal-inhibition interneurons, IaInt-F/IaInt-E proprioceptive
interneurons, and M-F/M-E motor pools --- over the last few cycles of
each 120\,s run. Three features are consistent across all five learning
modes and all conditions: (i) within each leg, every flexor-side
population fires in strict antiphase to its extensor-side counterpart;
(ii) the two legs hold a $180^{\circ}$ offset (right-leg panels, tinted,
are half a cycle out of phase with the left); and (iii) the reciprocal
InF/InE and IaInt interneurons carry the same clean alternation as the
rhythm generators, confirming that the inhibitory core --- not just the
motor read-out --- is phase-locked. The descending (Fig.~\ref{fig:net_speed})
and sensory-sited (Fig.~\ref{fig:net_sensory}) arms are essentially
indistinguishable at the population level across the speed range, and the
loading arms (Figs.~\ref{fig:net_ablstim}--\ref{fig:net_ablsens}) show the
same organisation at baseline loading; where it degrades under unloading
is quantified in \autoref{sec:results:ablation} and
\autoref{sec:results:sensory}.

\subsection{Graded sensory ablation: epidural stimulation rescues stepping under unloading}\label{sec:results:ablation}

\paragraph{Two loading models.}
Limb unloading attenuates two afferent channels at once: proprioceptive
Ia feedback (force and stretch) and cutaneous feedback (paw contact).
We therefore ran the graded-ablation experiment in two versions. In the
\emph{epidural-stim-assisted} version the paced cutaneous drive is held
at full amplitude regardless of loading --- the computational analogue
of epidural electrical stimulation, which supplies a rhythmic afferent
volley independent of limb
contact~\cite{lavrov2008,courtine2009,harkema2011} --- and only the
closed-loop Ia is attenuated. In the \emph{natural} (unassisted)
version both the cutaneous drive (via \verb|--cut-feedback-gain|) and
the Ia feedback are scaled together by the loading level
($G \in \{1.0, 0.5, 0.1\}$ for baseline / toe / air stepping). The
external Ia-E heel$\to$toe ramp that clocks the cycle is left at full
amplitude in both versions.

\paragraph{Anchor result: the paced cutaneous drive preserves stepping.}
The two versions are indistinguishable at full weight-bearing
(baseline: corr $-0.90$ in both) but diverge sharply as the limb is
unloaded (Fig.~\ref{fig:epidural}, at the baseline learning rate
$\lambda=10^{-3}$). Under the natural model the counter-phase
\emph{degrades markedly} --- from $-0.90$ (baseline) through $-0.77$ (toe) to
$-0.52$ (air stepping) --- with the cycle period becoming highly
irregular (s.d.\ $130$\,ms at air stepping), the extensor riding
tonically high and the flexor alternating erratically. Under the
epidural-stim-assisted model the same unloading leaves the rhythm intact
(air stepping: corr $-0.98$, period s.d.\ $0$\,ms). The paced cutaneous
drive --- the stimulation analogue --- is therefore what preserves
stepping once limb-contact feedback is lost, directly reproducing the
central finding of the Courtine/Lavrov SCI-rehabilitation program:
epidural lumbar stimulation enables treadmill stepping in spinal animals
that cannot otherwise step~\cite{lavrov2008,courtine2009,harkema2011}.

\paragraph{The natural arm across learning rates.}
We now characterise the \emph{natural} (unassisted) arm --- in which
both the cutaneous and the Ia feedback are gated by loading --- across
the full loading $\times$ learning-rate matrix
($\lambda \in \{1{\times}10^{-5},\,1{\times}10^{-4},\,1{\times}10^{-3}\}$),
at the medium-walk anchor (520\,ms cycle period, 120\,s simulations).
One relationship organises the matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablgrad},
Table~\ref{tab:ablgrad_metrics}): at full weight-bearing the rhythm
alternates strongly at every learning rate (corr $-0.85$ to $-0.95$),
but as the limb is unloaded the counter-phase degrades, and it degrades
\emph{least where the learned descending drive is most developed}. Under
air stepping the counter-phase strengthens monotonically with $\lambda$
--- from $-0.29$ at the slow rate (converged CUT$\to$RG-E only $4$\,pA)
through $-0.22$ at the intermediate rate to $-0.52$ at the baseline rate
(CUT$\to$RG-E $60$\,pA) --- and the same ordering holds under toe

cutaneous drive is \emph{itself} gated by loading, even the
fully-learned baseline rate cannot hold a clean rhythm under air stepping
($-0.52$); restoring that requires the ungated cutaneous drive of the
epidural-stim arm (Fig.~\ref{fig:epidural}, $-0.98$).

\paragraph{Interpretation: the learned descending drive compensates for
lost feedback.}
The matrix tells a single, coherent story that unifies Phase~B with the
epidural contrast above. As the limb unloads, the two afferent channels
that would otherwise sustain the rhythm --- cutaneous and Ia --- are both
attenuated; what remains to hold the gait is the STDP-learned descending
drive. The more that drive has developed (the higher the learning rate),
the better the rhythm survives unloading, so under air stepping the
counter-phase strengthens monotonically with $\lambda$
(Table~\ref{tab:ablgrad_metrics}). At full weight-bearing the learned
drive is not needed --- the intact afferents carry the rhythm and all
three rates alternate strongly --- and under progressive unloading the
learning rate becomes the decisive variable: the fully-converged baseline
rate ($\lambda=10^{-3}$, CUT$\to$RG-E $\approx 60$\,pA) is the most
robust, while the barely-developed slow rate ($\lambda=10^{-5}$,
$\approx 4$\,pA) is the most fragile --- its peak force collapsing to
$\approx 10$\,a.u.\ and its period losing lock ($230\pm97$\,ms). Where
the rhythm does hold, peak force is preserved, so the degradation is
primarily a \emph{timing} effect. The ceiling on this compensation is
intrinsic to the natural arm: because its cutaneous drive is gated too,
learning can only \emph{partially} substitute for lost feedback --- which
is precisely the gap the exogenous epidural-stim drive fills.

\paragraph{Frozen-weight control: does the imposed distribution suffice?}
A residual question concerns the clean rhythm at full weight-bearing: is
it carried merely by the \emph{marginal} statistics of the converged
weights (their mean and spread), or by finer learning-induced structure?
To isolate this we re-simulated \emph{baseline} loading with STDP switched off
(\verb|--stdp-lambda 0|) and the CUT$\to$RG-E weights drawn from a
prescribed lognormal of fixed mean and coefficient of variation (CV),
BS$\to$RG-E scaled to the observed $0.25\times$ ratio, all other synapses
at the bio-plausible defaults (Fig.~\ref{fig:frozen}). Two controlled
sweeps isolate the candidate mechanisms: a \emph{mean} sweep at fixed
tight spread (the weakness axis --- does a weak imposed drive give a
clean rhythm and an intermediate one degrade it?) and a \emph{CV} sweep
at fixed full mean (the heterogeneity axis --- does broadening the
imposed distribution toward the converged spread, $\mathrm{CV}\approx
0.05$, recover the clean rhythm?). The plastic reference to reproduce is
the natural arm at baseline loading, corr $-0.90$ ($\lambda=10^{-3}$)
with converged CUT$\to$RG-E mean $\approx63$\,pA.
Across the mean sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:frozen}a) the frozen rhythm is
strong throughout but strikingly non-monotonic: correlation is cleanest
at the \emph{weakest} imposed mean (corr $-0.99$ at $15$\,pA, weaker
than the plastic reference), dips to its worst in the middle of the
range ($-0.73$ to $-0.74$ at $35$--$45$\,pA), then partially recovers
at the full converged mean ($-0.88$ at $63$\,pA, tight CV$=0.02$).
Peak extensor force is essentially flat across the entire sweep
($16.71$--$16.72$\,a.u.), so the imposed CUT$\to$RG-E weight does not
gate \emph{whether} the rhythm exists at baseline loading --- the
intact BS tonic drive, closed-loop Ia feedback, and cutaneous pacing
ramp already carry most of it --- it mainly modulates phase-locking
\emph{quality}. The CV sweep at the full mean (Fig.~\ref{fig:frozen}b)
is comparatively flat ($-0.80$ to $-0.87$ across CV $0.02$--$0.10$) and
non-monotonic in the same way; broadening the spread toward the
converged heterogeneity does not recover the plastic reference ---
imposing the true converged marginal (mean $63$\,pA, CV$\approx0.05$,
interpolated corr $\approx-0.81$) falls \emph{short} of the plastic
$-0.90$ by about $0.09$, while the tightest imposed spread at the same
mean (CV$=0.02$, corr $-0.88$) comes within $0.02$ of it. So hand-tuning
the exact converged CV does not close the gap and if anything goes the
wrong way: the marginal distribution alone --- even matched in mean and
spread --- does not fully reproduce the plastic baseline rhythm, so STDP
organises weight \emph{structure} (which specific synapses are
strengthened, correlated with each afferent's phase) beyond what its
marginal mean and coefficient of variation capture.

\paragraph{Relation to SCI rehabilitation.}
The paired contrast (Fig.~\ref{fig:epidural}) gives the model a direct
SCI-rehabilitation reading. When the paced cutaneous drive is intact
(the epidural-stimulation analogue), the spinal CPG maintains a stable
counter-phase rhythm despite severely attenuated proprioceptive feedback
across the entire loading range. When that drive is gated by loading
(the natural, unassisted case), the rhythm collapses as the limb is
unloaded. This double dissociation reproduces the mechanism underlying
the clinical observations of Courtine 2009 and Harkema
2011~\cite{courtine2009,harkema2011}: chronic SCI rats and humans with
motor-complete paraplegia cannot step unassisted but recover treadmill
stepping --- from weight-bearing through to limb-suspended air stepping
--- under epidural lumbar stimulation. In the model, the stimulation
substitutes for the lost loading-dependent afferent drive, exactly the
function the cutaneous pacing serves here.

\subsection{Sensory-sited plasticity: relocating the learning to the proprioceptive loop}\label{sec:results:sensory}

\paragraph{Freezing the descending plasticity, adding a plastic Ia$\to$RG loop.}
In all experiments so far the plasticity lived in the \emph{descending}
projections (cutaneous CUT$\to$RG-E and brainstem BS$\to$RG). We now ask
whether the learning must be sited there, or whether it can instead be
carried by the \emph{sensory} loop. We froze both BS$\to$RG projections
as static synapses at their weak lognormal initialisation (removing all
descending brainstem plasticity) and added a plastic homonymous
proprioceptive projection --- Ia-E$\to$RG-E and Ia-F$\to$RG-F --- so that
the muscle afferents become the learning input to the rhythm generators.
Because this loop is in-phase positive feedback, its weight cap is
critical: a low cap ($w_{\max}=10$\,pA) lets a light \emph{phased} boost
reinforce each burst without filling the inter-burst trough, whereas a
high cap saturates into tonic co-excitation of both half-centres that
abolishes counter-phase (corr$(F_E,F_F)$ falls monotonically from
$-0.98$ at $w_{\max}=10$ to $+0.12$ at $w_{\max}=120$). The plastic
Ia$\to$RG weight self-stabilises at only $\approx4.5$\,pA --- well below
the cap --- a genuine low-gain equilibrium rather than a clamp.

\paragraph{At normal loading, relocation is cost-free.}
Across the full speed range the sensory-learning model matches the
descending control on counter-phase (last 20\,s, $\lambda=10^{-3}$):
corr$(F_E,F_F) = -0.99,\,-0.98,\,-0.96$ for the sensory arm at
$6/13.5/21$\,cm/s versus $-0.97,\,-0.88,\,-0.95$ for the descending arm,
with identical force amplitudes ($16$--$17$\,a.u., balanced E/F) and the
same load set-point on the still-plastic CUT$\to$RG-E weight
($\approx63$\,pA). The descending arm's brainstem contribution is small
to begin with (BS$\to$RG converges to only $\approx15$\,pA against its
$30$\,pA cap, the bulk of the drive being cutaneous); the sensory arm
replaces that small plastic descending term with an even lighter
$\approx4.5$\,pA \emph{phased proprioceptive} term and loses nothing.
The learning trajectory is likewise preserved: CUT$\to$RG-E climbs to the
same attractor and the slow $\lambda=10^{-5}$ transient is still rising
at 120\,s, exactly as in the descending arm
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sensory}a).

\paragraph{Under unloading, sensory plasticity is partially self-rescuing.}
The decisive difference appears under limb unloading, where we now have a
\emph{three-way} contrast at the medium-walk anchor: the epidural-stim
arm (cutaneous drive intact), the natural arm (cutaneous drive gated by
loading), and the new sensory-learning arm (Table~\ref{tab:sensory},
$\lambda=10^{-3}$). At full weight-bearing all three are clean. Under
\emph{toe} stepping ($G=0.5$) the sensory arm preserves the rhythm
(corr $-0.99$) where the natural arm has already degraded ($-0.77$): the
plastic Ia$\to$RG loop reinforces the pattern from the proprioception
that is still present. Under \emph{air} stepping ($G=0.1$), however, the
sensory arm degrades to $-0.51$ --- essentially tied with the natural
arm ($-0.52$) and well short of the stim arm ($-0.98$). This is the
central limit of sensory-sited learning: under total unloading the Ia
feedback is the very input the pathway learns from, so it cannot
bootstrap a pattern from proprioception it no longer receives, and its
advantage over the unassisted natural arm vanishes. Epidural-stim drive,
being exogenous to the sensory loss, is unaffected. The sensory arm's
gain is therefore specific to \emph{partial} unloading (toe stepping),
where residual proprioception still carries a learnable pattern.

\paragraph{Mechanism and rehabilitation reading.}
The three arms separate at the extensor (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensory}c). With
the stim drive intact the extensor half-centre rate is held or rises with
unloading (RG-E mean $418\to458\to487$\,Hz); in the natural arm it
collapses ($419\to267\to128$\,Hz) as the flexor takes over
($197\to222\to257$\,Hz); the sensory arm's extensor rate falls with the
natural collapse ($419\to261\to78$\,Hz) yet it keeps
the E/F \emph{force} balanced down to toe stepping, and recovers extensor
force at air stepping only for fast learning ($\lambda=10^{-3}$: peak
$F_E=16.7$ vs $\lambda=10^{-5}$: $8.1$\,a.u.). Read against
SCI-rehabilitation physiology, the three arms reproduce a graded
hierarchy: load-independent epidural stimulation restores stepping across
the full unloading range~\cite{courtine2009,harkema2011}; proprioceptive
reinforcement improves weight-supported (toe) stepping but cannot
substitute for load feedback during suspension~\cite{edgerton2008,rossignol2006};
and passive descending drive without either loses the pattern as load is
withdrawn. The model thus makes a sharp statement: \emph{where the
plasticity is sited determines which rehabilitation regime it survives}
--- sensory-sited learning is self-rescuing under partial loading but
hostage to its own gated input under total unloading.

\subsection{Summary of results}\label{sec:results:summary}

Eight findings emerge across the experiments.
(1) STDP self-organises the descending and cutaneous weights to the
same attractor across four orders of magnitude of initialisation
(\autoref{sec:results:stdp}).
(2) The resulting paced gait is stable over \SI{120}{\second} with no
drift in amplitude or period (\autoref{sec:results:stability}).
(3) The gait tracks the commanded step period across the rat locomotor
range (6--21\,cm/s) with sub-millisecond error; counter-phase remains
strong throughout ($|\mathrm{corr}|\ge0.81$) but depends
non-monotonically on the STDP rate --- cleanest at the slow rate for
slow and medium walk, and at the baseline rate for the fast gait
(\autoref{sec:results:speed}).
(4) A paired loading experiment dissociates the two afferent channels:
when the paced cutaneous drive is gated by loading (the natural,
unassisted case) the rhythm degrades markedly under air stepping
(corr $-0.52$), whereas holding that drive intact (the epidural-stim
analogue) preserves it (corr $-0.98$) --- a direct model of the
Courtine/Lavrov epidural-stimulation rescue
(\autoref{sec:results:ablation}, Fig.~\ref{fig:epidural}).
(5) In the natural (unassisted) arm, unloading degrades the rhythm, and
it does so \emph{least} where the learned descending drive is most
developed: under air stepping the counter-phase strengthens monotonically
with the STDP rate ($-0.29$ at the slow rate, CUT$\to$RG-E $\approx4$\,pA,
to $-0.52$ at the baseline rate, $\approx60$\,pA), so learning partially
compensates for lost feedback (\autoref{sec:results:ablation}).
(6) A frozen-weight control (STDP off, CUT$\to$RG-E drawn from a
prescribed lognormal at baseline loading) shows the rhythm's
\emph{existence} is robust to the imposed CUT$\to$RG-E weight (force
output unchanged across a $15$--$63$\,pA sweep) but its \emph{quality}
is not: the converged marginal distribution (mean $63$\,pA,
CV$\approx0.05$) falls short of the plastic reference by $\approx0.09$
correlation, so STDP organises weight structure beyond the marginal
mean and spread (\autoref{sec:results:ablation}, Fig.~\ref{fig:frozen}).
(7) The plasticity can be relocated from the descending pathway to the
proprioceptive loop --- freezing BS$\to$RG and adding a light
($\approx4.5$\,pA) plastic Ia$\to$RG projection --- with no loss of
counter-phase at normal loading across the entire speed range
(\autoref{sec:results:sensory}, Fig.~\ref{fig:sensory}).
(8) Under unloading the three learning loci form a hierarchy: the
sensory-sited model self-rescues \emph{toe} stepping (corr $-0.99$ vs
natural $-0.77$) but under \emph{air} stepping degrades to $-0.51$ ---
tied with the natural arm ($-0.52$) --- because its Ia learning drive is
itself gated away, whereas the load-independent epidural-stim drive
remains robust ($-0.98$) --- so where the plasticity is sited determines
which rehabilitation regime it survives
(\autoref{sec:results:sensory}, Table~\ref{tab:sensory}).
