\subsection{Self-organisation of locomotor pattern via STDP}\label{sec:results:stdp}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
Across all ten initial-weight points
$(\mu,\mathrm{CV}) \in \{(0,0),\,(0.5,0.8),\,(1.0,0.6),\,(2.0,0.45),\,
(3.5,0.30),\,(5.0,0.20),\,(7.0,0.15),\,(9.0,0.10),\,(12.0,0.08),\,
(16.0,0.05)\}$, all three plastic projections (CUT$\to$RG-E, BS$\to$RG-E,
BS$\to$RG-F) converge to the same steady-state weight distribution by
$t \approx 5{-}10\,\mathrm{s}$ and remain stable for the full
\SI{120}{\second} of simulation.

\paragraph{Quantitative summary.}
\todo{Write 1-2 paragraphs around the following numbers, all reported
on the last 20\,s window:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Mean CUT$\to$RG-E weight: $\sim 65\,\mathrm{pA}$
        (steady plateau, independent of $\mu$, CV).
  \item Mean BS$\to$RG-E weight: $\sim 15\,\mathrm{pA}$
        (saturated at the BS-specific ceiling $W_{\max}=30\,\mathrm{pA}$).
  \item Mean BS$\to$RG-F weight: $\sim 16\,\mathrm{pA}$.
  \item Trajectory dependence on init: the smallest initial weights
        ($\mu=0$) take $\sim 5\,\mathrm{s}$ longer to reach plateau than
        the largest ($\mu=16$), but the asymptote is indistinguishable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Long-term stability of counter-phase locomotion}\label{sec:results:stability}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
Over \SI{120}{\second} (120 gait cycles) at the production
configuration ($\mu=3.5$, $\mathrm{CV}=0.30$, BS\,$=60\,$Hz, N$=$200 per
half-centre), Force-E peaks at \SI{17.4}{\au}, Force-F at
\SI{17.4}{\au}, with a Force-E$\leftrightarrow$Force-F Pearson
correlation of $-0.91$ on the last 20\,s window. Cycle period jitter
is $<\SI{1}{\milli\second}$.

  \item Counter-phase Pearson $r$: $-0.91$
  \item Mean Force-E peak-to-peak period over the last 20\,s:

  \item L vs R phase offset: $180^{\circ} \pm 2^{\circ}$

\subsection{Ablation study: necessity of each component}\label{sec:results:ablation}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
We tested five ablation conditions at the same operating point
($\mu=3.5$, $\mathrm{CV}=0.30$, 30\,s each):
\emph{baseline} (all features on), \emph{noIa} (Ia$\to$reciprocal-IN
removed), \emph{symInh} (reciprocal inhibition made symmetric),
\emph{noComm} (commissural inhibition removed), \emph{noPaced} (paced
drive replaced by legacy rotating cutaneous stimulation).
Only two ablations broke the gait: \textbf{noComm}, which lost L$\leftrightarrow$R
desynchronisation and weakened E$\leftrightarrow$F counter-phase, and
\textbf{noPaced}, which collapsed the rhythm to fast and noisy oscillation.

\paragraph{Interpretation: redundancy under paced drive.}
\todo{1 paragraph framing the surprising finding: removing the Ia
closed loop or the Zhang asymmetry does \emph{not} significantly degrade
the paced-gait pattern. Reason: the paced external Ia drive
($60{\to}80{\to}100\,$Hz sequential ramp) functionally substitutes for
the internal Ia$\to$reciprocal-IN loop, and the strong commissural and
external pacing dominate the asymmetric reciprocal inhibition. This is
consistent with the deafferentation literature
(\cite{pearson1995,rossignol2006}) showing that residual descending
drive plus reciprocal inhibition is sufficient when sensory feedback
is intact.}

\paragraph{Interpretation: what is required.}
\todo{1 paragraph: the two components that \emph{are} required are
(i)~commissural inhibition --- without it, L$\leftrightarrow$R alternation
breaks down, and the counter-phase strength drops from $-0.91$ to $-0.49$;
(ii)~paced external drive --- without it, the rhythm collapses to
${\sim}400$\,ms cycles with high jitter
(s.d.\ $\approx 100\,$ms) and counter-phase correlation degenerates
to $-0.16$. Together these results identify commissural inhibition as
the load-bearing central component, and paced sensory drive as the
load-bearing closed-loop component.}

\subsection{Speed sweep: gait tracks commanded step period}\label{sec:results:speed}

\paragraph{Anchor result.}
Across the rat locomotor range
(\verb|--step-period-ms| $\in$ \{600, 800, 1000, 1200, 1400\}\,ms),
measured cycle period tracks commanded period within
$<\SI{0.5}{\milli\second}$. Counter-phase strength stays in
$[-0.91, -0.83]$ across all speeds. Peak Force-E rises monotonically
from 17.02 to 17.47\,a.u.\ as the step period increases.

\paragraph{Interpretation: rise-time limitation at fast gaits.}
\todo{1 paragraph noting that at the fastest commanded step (600\,ms),
Force-E peak is $\sim 17.02$ vs.\ $17.47\,$a.u.\ at slowest (1400\,ms) ---
a $\sim$3\,\% compression. This is consistent with rat fast-twitch
fibre rise time ($\tau_F=80$\,ms) becoming a non-negligible fraction
of the 250\,ms stance window, producing a small but measurable
under-development of peak force per stride. Bio-plausible Hill-like
saturation~\cite{zajac1989}.}

\paragraph{Interpretation: bio-plausible range.}
\todo{1 paragraph relating the cycle period range to mouse/rat treadmill
data \cite{bellaritakiehn2015,lemieux2016} --- our 600\,ms cycle maps to
${\sim}30\,$cm/s rat trot, 1400\,ms to slow walk. Counter-phase
correlation remains strong ($\le -0.83$) across the full range,
indicating that paced sensory drive can support the rat locomotor speed
range without retuning of the central architecture.}

\subsection{Summary of results}\label{sec:results:summary}

\todo{Brief 1-paragraph synthesis summarising the three main findings:
(1) STDP self-organises descending/cutaneous weights to the same
attractor across four orders of magnitude in initialisation;
(2) the resulting paced gait is stable over 120\,s with no drift;
(3) commissural inhibition and paced drive are the two
load-bearing components, while Ia closed-loop and asymmetric
reciprocal inhibition are partially redundant under paced drive;
(4) cycle period tracks commanded step period across the rat
locomotor range with near-zero error.}
